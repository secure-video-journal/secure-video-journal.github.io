Memory inventory: a journaling exercise for negative self-beliefs

Write down what you believe about yourself, then list the moments that prove it wrong

Published: Jun 7, 2026 • Updated: Oct 26, 2026
The InnerArc team

A memory inventory is a journaling exercise for correcting negative self-beliefs: you write down a belief you hold about yourself, then list real moments from your own life that contradict it.

Most of us carry a quiet belief about who we are. Sometimes it’s a sentence, like I’m too quiet or I’m bad with money. More often it’s an assumption working in the background, deciding whether you speak up in a meeting or how early you leave a party. It feels like plain fact, so it rarely gets questioned, and over the years it can shrink what you’re willing to try. A memory inventory tests that belief against your own track record.

It helps most when you’re stuck in a familiar bit of self-criticism, when you’re avoiding something because of how you expect to handle it, or when the same doubt keeps turning up in different parts of your life.

There are no affirmations involved. The aim is to remember your past accurately, including the good parts you’ve been leaving out.

What goes into a memory inventory

The inventory is a collection of specific moments from your past, chosen because they push against a narrow or negative view of yourself, and each one has to be something that really happened.

It’s a different thing from a gratitude list, and it doesn’t ask you to play down real failures. You keep the bad moments and add back the ones you’d forgotten. You won’t come out of it believing you’re perfect, and that’s fine, because the point is to stop treating half the picture as the whole of it.

It’s also harder than most journaling. Morning pages are close to automatic writing, and a morning intention or an evening recap takes a few minutes, but this one asks you to look back and think hard. To be honest I’ve struggled with it, but it’s the practice that has given me the most. A morning entry clears my head for the day, while a memory inventory has shifted how I see myself in a way that lasts. It’s hard enough that I only come back to it every few weeks, and that turns out to be often enough.

Why the bad moments stick

The way other people see you is usually a long way from the way you see yourself, and it’s your own view that decides how you act. In a 2018 study in Psychological Science, Erica Boothby and colleagues paired up strangers for a conversation. Afterwards people consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them and enjoyed their company, and the shyer they were, the more they underestimated it.

I’ve labelled myself shy and introverted for years. When a moment comes up where I could speak up and I don’t, I’m left thinking I should have, and that moment sticks far harder than any good memory, so I dwell on it and the label digs in deeper. Meanwhile all the times I did speak up fade into the background.

That pattern is well documented. In a 2001 review in the Review of General Psychology, Roy Baumeister and colleagues found that bad events and negative feedback have a stronger, longer-lasting effect on us than good ones of the same size. Once a belief takes hold, you notice the memories that fit it and wave away the rest, until it feels like a fact simply because it’s familiar.

A memory inventory pulls those background moments forward so the evidence is balanced again. Cognitive therapists use a version of this: Christine Padesky described a “positive data log”, where you record everyday evidence that contradicts a negative core belief. This is a private, journal version of that. It isn’t therapy, and if a belief comes from something heavier, talking to a professional is the right move.

Seeing the moments written down or spoken aloud tends to make the pattern clearer than turning them over in your head ever does.

How do you challenge a negative self-belief?

Write the belief down, pick a stretch of time, and collect the moments from it that contradict the belief. Here’s the full exercise, with the example from my own memory inventory for shy.

1. Name the belief. Pick one sentence that feels familiar, the kind you’d mutter to yourself, and nothing dramatic. Write it down exactly as it sounds in your head, for example I’m shy and introverted, I’m bad at speaking up or I struggle in social situations.

2. Choose a time window. Pick a set period, such as the last year or one chapter of your life, so you’re searching real weeks and months instead of vague impressions.

3. Collect concrete counter-examples. List specific moments that contradict the belief. They should be things you did, with as much detail as you can remember. When I did this for shy, my list included:

  • speaking up in a work meeting about something I believed in strongly
  • joking around with my family and extended family, joining in the banter and getting loud
  • long one-to-one conversations where I felt a real connection with the other person
  • joining a new cycling club and a climbing social full of people I’d never met, and happily talking away because we had something in common

Write them down without explaining or weighing them yet.

4. Sit with the pattern. Read the list slowly and notice what surprised you and which examples are hard to argue with. If it feels uncomfortable, that’s usually a good sign, because it’s what an old belief feels like when it starts to come loose.

5. Revisit it later. Come back after a few weeks and ask whether the belief still feels complete, and what a more accurate version would sound like. Swapping shy for outgoing would be another half-truth. Something like I’m quiet in some rooms and I talk easily when I care about the subject is much closer to the picture my list gave me.

Give it a quiet space

This exercise needs somewhere quiet where nobody is judging you, including you, so I wouldn’t try it in the living room with the TV on. It can bring up hurtful memories, because the bad moments are why the belief exists, and memory is imperfect, so the good times can take real digging. Go gently.

A few things to watch for:

  • Stay with specific moments and resist generalising.
  • Don’t argue with yourself. Let the list do the work.
  • Don’t rush to a conclusion, because the insight often turns up days later.

If it feels flat the first time, give it another go in a few weeks, because the effect builds each time you come back.

Doing it out loud

Some people find this easier spoken aloud, and I’m one of them. Writing puts a filter between the thought and the page: part of you is checking the spelling and the grammar and how it reads, and your hand never keeps up with your head. For most journaling that’s a small cost. Here it matters more, because you’re digging for memories, and one tends to lead to the next when you’re talking freely. Speaking goes at the speed of thought, so you can follow a memory where it goes instead of stopping to write the last one down.

Open a recorder on your phone and start. There’s no page to fill and nothing to spell, and if you get stuck you can sit quietly until the next memory turns up.

Listening back is where the voice version pays off most. Your voice carries hesitation and certainty that don’t make it onto the page, and when you play a recording a few weeks later those cues often show which beliefs still hold and which have started to give. If you’ve never journaled by voice, there’s more on how to journal when you hate writing and on doing morning pages out loud.

Common questions

How often should you do a memory inventory? Every few weeks is plenty. It sits well alongside morning pages or a weekly review, and it’s worth coming back to whenever an old label starts getting loud again.

Is a memory inventory the same as affirmations? No. Affirmations ask you to repeat something positive whether or not you believe it. A memory inventory only uses things that really happened, so the bad moments stay on the list too.

Can a memory inventory replace therapy? No. It’s a private journaling exercise based on a technique cognitive therapists use, and it works best for everyday self-criticism. If a belief is tied to something heavier, or it keeps you from living the way you want, talk to a professional.
