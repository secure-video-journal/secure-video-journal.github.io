If you hate writing, talk instead, and record yourself on whatever device you already have with you, whether that's your phone or your laptop. To be honest, "hate" is a strong word for how I feel about writing, but there's real friction in it for me, and speaking takes almost all of that friction away.

## Notebooks with weeks between entries

During lockdown I journaled in notepads, and those were some of my most productive writing months, mostly because I had so much time on my hands. Since then the notepads have piled up. I have several journals on the go, each with sparse entries and no system linking them, so if I picked one up today I'd have to flick through them all to find the one with the latest entry. I know for a fact that entry would be weeks, if not months, old.

What worked instead was already sitting in front of me. I work from home and my laptop is always with me, so one day I opened Photo Booth, started a recording and spoke my mind. I saved each file on my desktop with the date in its name, and after a while I had a folder full of entries in date order. Later I wanted to do the same on the move, when I was driving or walking somewhere on my own, so I pulled out my phone, opened the notes app and spoke out loud. That worked too.

## The blank page is a hurdle

Sitting down in the morning with a pen in your hand and a blank page in front of you is hard. Something about that big empty white space makes it difficult to start, and it's a hurdle you have to get over every time you sit down.

Anything you want to make a habit has a better chance of sticking when there are fewer hurdles in the way. The psychologist Wendy Wood makes this case in [*Good Habits, Bad Habits*](https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781250159076) (2019), where her research shows that small amounts of friction do a lot to decide which habits stick and which fade. Finding the notepad and checking the pen works are exactly that kind of friction, and opening a recorder on a device you're already holding removes them.

## The filter between thought and ink

The bigger hurdle for me is the filter that sits between a thought in your head and ink on the paper. We spend years at school learning to write, and that training doesn't switch off when you journal. Some of it is conscious and some of it isn't, but part of you is always checking the spelling and the grammar and trying to make the page look nice, and on top of that there's the physical work of moving your hand across the page.

All of that takes time, so writing is slower than speaking, and your writing will never catch up with your thoughts however fast you are. I've tried many times. Speaking goes at the speed of thought, because talking is how we naturally communicate. When I recorded myself there was no notepad to find and nothing to spell, so the ideas came out as fast as I had them. Voice journals have been the easiest way I've found to lower the barrier to journaling and the mental effort it takes.

## The smallest version that still counts

Pick the device you spend the most time on and record your thoughts out loud. There are two ways to do it, and either one is enough.

**An evening recap.** At the end of the day, talk through what was good about it and what you wish you'd done better. This sits close to one of the best-tested exercises in positive psychology. In a 2005 study in *American Psychologist*, [Martin Seligman and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16045394/) asked people to write down three things that went well each evening for a week, and doing so raised their happiness and lowered their depressive symptoms for up to six months.

**A morning entry.** Start the day by saying what would make today great, a prompt popularised by The Five Minute Journal, and then say what you're looking forward to. Your answer to the first question has to be something you can control. Good weather is out of your hands, so pick something like taking a proper lunch break away from your desk.

## What it changed for me

Those two prompts were enough to make real changes for me during lockdown. Speaking them helped me change behaviours in my own character that I'd wanted to change for a while, and it helped me see the good things in my days that I might otherwise have missed.
