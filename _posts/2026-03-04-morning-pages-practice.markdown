Yes, you can do morning pages out loud. When I started talking my morning pages instead of writing them, the filter of having to come up with something with quality kind of dropped away, and it went a lot easier.

## Where morning pages come from

Morning pages come from Julia Cameron's 1992 book *The Artist's Way*. Her method is three pages of longhand writing, done first thing in the morning as a stream of consciousness, and you aren't meant to reread them.

I actually first heard about it from the Rich Roll podcast. He's got a lot of practices around mindfulness that I really rate, and this was one of them that he recommended doing.

## My first go, on paper

I tried it written in my journal, which is a lovely Japanese lined paper notebook. Shamefully, it has very sparse entries. At this point they're probably weeks and months apart.

I did it one whole morning. I sat in my kitchen at the dining table before the kids woke up for school, with my hot coffee by my side, and gave it a go.

To be honest, I kind of struggled. I understand the concept, and it's very similar to automatic drawing in that you're just not supposed to think. You're supposed to let the thoughts flow out of you and capture whatever comes out.

But the practice of it was really hard, because when I'm writing I tend to really think about what I'm writing. I consciously make an effort to put pen to paper with something that has some quality. So I couldn't really get the automatic style of writing, the flowiness of it that I think was intended. That probably does come with practice, but for me, I wrote it in a careful way, and I probably got about 1.5 to 2 pages written.

The things I was thinking about at 7am, as I was groggy and the kids were slowly waking up, were basically what was coming in the day and what I needed to do. There were my chores for the day and stuff I was worried about. Maybe there was a little bit about how my sleep went the night before. There were the Slack messages I needed to respond to and the emails that came in through the night. Mostly it was my general tasks for the day ahead.

I don't think that's the intention of the thing. I think it's meant to be more stream of consciousness, and the rambling-ness of it is where the value is.

## Speaking them instead

When I started to talk the morning pages, that filter of having to come up with something with quality dropped away. It went a lot easier, and I started talking about random stuff, like my dreams or restlessness in the night. The day ahead came up less. There were thoughts about the kids and school drop-offs, and how I felt about my own drop-offs when I was a kid.

Speaking does break Cameron's rule about longhand, and I did wonder whether it still counts. I think in some ways the intention is hit even harder by speaking, because that filter is taken away: the worry that I'm wasting paper, I'm wasting ink, my journal needs to look pretty. So in a way I feel like the speaking aspect of it really opens it up.

## How to do it tomorrow morning

If a friend told me they'd tried journaling but struggled to write and to keep it consistent, this is what I'd say.

Wake up tomorrow and open a recording app on your phone. Make sure you're somewhere quiet and alone, and just speak.

The hardest bit is starting, so always begin with something like "It's Tuesday morning and I'm sitting in my kitchen", or the date and where you are, just to get you flowing a little.

Then just speak your mind. Say whatever you're thinking about. It could be your chores for the day or how you slept last night. It could be that thing you watched last night that made you remember a childhood memory.

It doesn't need to be a journal, and it doesn't need to be about what's happening right now. You don't even need to revisit it. But what comes out of it might surprise you, and it's always super interesting to read them back weeks or months after you recorded them.

## What I get out of it

The value is in getting that stuff out of your mind so that in your day you can start fresh on new thoughts. You don't need to dwell on things you didn't realise were in your head but actually were. There's probably some really clever psychology behind all of that, about the things in your subconscious mind that you can let escape by talking about them, so that you can start free. I don't know what that's called, but that's the value I feel from this practice.
