## Where morning pages come from

Morning pages come from Julia Cameron's 1992 book *The Artist's Way*. Her method is three pages of longhand writing, done first thing in the morning as a stream of consciousness, and you aren't meant to reread them.

I first heard about them on the Rich Roll podcast. He talks about a lot of mindfulness practices that I really rate, and morning pages was one he recommended.

## My first go, on paper

I tried them in my journal, a lovely Japanese notebook with lined paper which, shamefully, has entries weeks or even months apart. I sat at the kitchen table one morning before the kids woke up for school, with a hot coffee beside me, and gave it a go.

To be honest, I struggled. I understood the idea, which is a lot like automatic drawing: you aren't supposed to think, just let the thoughts flow out and capture whatever comes. In practice it was hard, because when I write I think carefully about every line and try to put something of quality on the page. I never found the loose, automatic flow that I think Cameron intended. That probably comes with practice, but I wrote carefully and managed about a page and a half.

At 7am, groggy, with the kids slowly waking up, what came out was mostly the day ahead: chores, things I was worried about, how I'd slept, the Slack messages and overnight emails waiting for a reply. I don't think that's the point of the practice. The value is meant to be in the rambling, and I was writing a to-do list.

## Speaking them instead

When I started speaking my morning pages instead, that pressure disappeared and it all came much more easily. I found myself talking about my dreams and a restless night, and much less about the day ahead. I talked about the kids and the school run, and then about how I felt about my own school drop-offs when I was a kid.

Speaking does break Cameron's rule about longhand, and I did wonder whether it still counts. I think it gets closer to what she was after, because it removes the little worries about wasting paper and ink and keeping the journal looking nice. For me, speaking opened the practice up.

## How to do it tomorrow morning

If a friend told me they'd tried journaling but couldn't stick with writing, this is what I'd tell them.

When you wake up, open a recording app on your phone, find somewhere quiet where you're alone, and start talking. Starting is the hardest part, so begin with the day and where you are, something like "It's Tuesday morning and I'm sitting in the kitchen", and let that get you going.

Then say whatever is on your mind. It might be the jobs for the day, how you slept, or something you watched last night that brought back a childhood memory. It doesn't have to be about what's happening now, and you don't need to listen back. What comes out might surprise you, though, and reading back recordings from weeks or months ago is often fascinating.

## Why it works

The point is to get those thoughts out of your head so you can start the day fresh, without dwelling on things you didn't realise were in there.

There's research behind that feeling. Psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished tasks keep pulling at your attention until you make a plan for them, and that simply writing the plan down was enough to quiet them (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2011). That's the Slack messages and the emails from my first morning: once they're out of your head, they stop circling.

Speaking counts too. Most of the research on putting feelings into words uses writing, but a 1994 study by Brian Esterling and colleagues compared writing with talking into a tape recorder, and the people who talked showed at least as much benefit (*Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*). So if a pen is what stops you, a voice recorder is a fair swap.

So in a way I feel like the speaking aspect of it really opens it up.

## How to do it tomorrow morning

If a friend told me they'd tried journaling but struggled to write and to keep it consistent, this is what I'd say.
